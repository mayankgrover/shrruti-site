What the research says — and what it does not

Acupuncture is a complementary therapy sometimes used by people with multiple sclerosis. A 2014 review of 12 studies suggested possible improvement in quality of life, fatigue, spasticity and pain, but the study designs were too weak for firm conclusions. A 2018 review found there was not enough evidence that acupuncture could reduce spasticity in MS. A 2021 review found traditional and scalp acupuncture appeared to help symptoms including fatigue, pain, walking difficulties and bladder problems. A 2021 randomised trial of 66 people found no significant difference in health-related quality of life between real acupuncture, sham acupuncture and the comparison group.

So on this site, acupuncture for MS is written as support — comfort, fatigue and quality of life — and never as a cure or a replacement for your neurologist.

  • Multiple Sclerosis Trust, “Acupuncture” (A–Z of MS), last updated 1 April 2022 — mstrust.org.uk/a-z/acupuncture (read 13 November 2026).
  • NCCIH (US National Institutes of Health), “Acupuncture: Effectiveness and Safety” — nccih.nih.gov (read 13 November 2026).
  • National Medical Commission, “Guidelines on Ethical Advertising and Public Communication”, 6 October 2026 — nmc.org.in (read 13 November 2026).

For painResearch shows acupuncture may be helpful for back or neck pain and for knee pain linked to osteoarthritis. A 2017 American College of Physicians guideline listed acupuncture among the non-drug options recommended as first-line for chronic low-back pain. For sciatica, reviews conclude it may be helpful, but the quality of the research is not good enough for definite conclusions.

On safetyRelatively few complications from acupuncture have been reported, and complications have resulted from non-sterile needles and improper delivery. Acupuncture needles are regulated as medical devices and are required to be sterile and for single use only.

Questions people ask

Plain answers, before you come.

What happens in the first consultation?

You talk through your symptoms, medical history and medicines, and what you want to be able to do again. If a treatment is appropriate, you may have it in the same visit. You leave with a written plan.

Is acupuncture safe, and what should I tell her first?

Relatively few complications from acupuncture have been reported, and complications have resulted from non-sterile needles and improper delivery; needles are regulated as medical devices and are required to be sterile and for single use only. Tell Dr. Grover about your medicines, any bleeding or clotting problem, pregnancy, or a pacemaker before your visit — she will go through this with you anyway. Source: NCCIH, “Acupuncture: Effectiveness and Safety” (read 13 November 2026).

Does acupuncture treat multiple sclerosis?

No. It is not a cure for MS, and it does not replace disease-modifying treatment or your neurologist. Research on acupuncture in MS is limited and mixed, so Dr. Grover uses acupuncture for MS support — comfort and quality of life — alongside your own treatment. The evidence note on this page links to the source. Source: Multiple Sclerosis Trust, “Acupuncture” (read 13 November 2026).

How many sessions will I need?

It depends on what you are coming with, how long it has been there, and how your body responds. Dr. Grover will tell you honestly what she thinks is reasonable after the first consultation, and what the research does and does not support. Nothing is promised before you have been assessed.
